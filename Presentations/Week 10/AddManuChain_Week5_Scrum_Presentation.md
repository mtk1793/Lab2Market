## SLIDE 2: BUSINESS THESIS

### Value Proposition Statement (≤100 words)

> Mid-size industrial operators in oil & gas, maritime, naval, and utilities — organizations that cannot absorb the capital burden of massive physical spare parts inventories yet cannot afford days of equipment downtime — face a slow, opaque, and fragmented spare parts sourcing process made worse by legacy ERP systems that don't know what they actually have in stock. **AddManuChain** replaces physical stockpiles with an AI-powered digital inventory platform that normalizes parts data across sources, connects operators to qualified additive manufacturing facilities on demand, and provides a Digital Birth Certificate guaranteeing full traceability — turning spare parts from a capital liability into a certified, intelligent, on-demand service.

---

## SLIDE 6: HYPOTHESIS TEST — SLIDE A

### Hypothesis NH.7: Certification Is the "Permission Layer" — the Only Remaining Barrier to AM Adoption

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Customer Segment** | Procurement managers, maintenance engineers, and operations leads at mid-size industrial operators |
| **Industry** | Oil & Gas, Maritime, Naval/Defense, Aerospace |
| **# of Interviews This Sprint Testing This Hyp.** | 9 |
| **Hypothesis** | The primary barrier to additive manufacturing adoption in regulated industries is not technical capability — AM already meets required specifications — it is the absence of a certified, traceable pathway from digital file to inspected, approved part. Without that "permission layer," operators cannot adopt AM regardless of technical readiness or financial motivation. |
| **Status** | ✅ **VALIDATED — Strong multi-source convergence** |

---

**Learnings & Insights:**

**1. Tyler Beatty (ExxonMobil, #33) — The Value-Capability-Permission Framework**
Tyler articulated the clearest synthesis of the entire 56-interview series. Industry already recognises the value (downtime = millions per day). Technical capability already exists. The single missing piece is regulatory permission: a certified, auditable, defensible process from digital file to manufactured part. This framework now organises AddManuChain's entire value proposition.

**2. Dave Whitehouse & Adam Saxty (Lloyd's Register, #45, #51)**
Lloyd's Register certifies *facilities and processes*, not individual parts. This is a workflow simplification that is architecturally central to the platform: operators do not need to independently certify each print job. They need to be connected to already-certified facilities. The Digital Birth Certificate is the traceability record of that process — not a new certification event each time.

**3. Scott Humber & Heather Davis (Aker Solutions, #57)**
Aker has a documented, mature global AM workflow. The Canada-specific gap is precise: no domestic platform connects the Aker procurement decision → qualified Canadian AM facility → Lloyd's Register certification milestone. The supply chain is broken specifically at the discovery-plus-certification-integration step, not at the manufacturing step.

**4. Cynthia Clark (WISC, #66)**
Certification must be designed in from day one — it cannot be retrofitted. Procurement managers in regulated industries will not adopt any tool that layers compliance on top of an existing process. The Digital Birth Certificate concept resonated immediately as the non-negotiable trust anchor.

**5. Trevor Butler (Lloyd's Register, #50)**
Offshore operators already ask Lloyd's Register informally for help sourcing and vetting suppliers. The certification body is functioning as an ad hoc supplier discovery service — confirming both the pain and the platform's opportunity to formalise that function.

## SLIDE 8: HYPOTHESIS TEST — SLIDE C

### Hypothesis AI.CORE: AI Is Not a Feature — It Is the Central Product Layer That Makes the Platform Commercially Viable

| Field | Content |
|---|---|
| **Customer Segment** | Procurement managers and supply chain leads at mid-size and large industrial operators |
| **Industry** | Oil & Gas, Maritime, Naval, Commercial Vehicles, Utilities |
| **# of Interviews This Sprint Testing This Hyp.** | 6 |
| **Hypothesis** | The most commercially viable near-term product is not the AM marketplace itself — it is the AI layer that sits underneath it: normalising messy multi-source inventory data, identifying what a company actually has vs. what it thinks it has, flagging AM-suitable parts, and ultimately connecting a predictive-maintenance signal to an automated sourcing trigger. This AI layer has standalone commercial value and builds the data relationships that enable the full platform. |
| **Status** | ✅ **STRONGLY VALIDATED — Confirmed across multiple sectors and roles** |

---

**Learnings & Insights:**

**1. Cynthia Clark (WISC, #66) — The "Tomato Problem": The Uncracked AI Use Case**
This is the single most commercially significant insight from the entire sprint. Cindy described, in precise operational detail, a problem she has encountered universally across every industry she has worked in — and has never seen any startup solve properly.

The problem: large organisations procure the same physical component from multiple suppliers over time. Each supplier has their own part number. Each internal system catalogues it differently. Over time, one alternator might exist in the inventory system under 8 or 10 different identifiers, each treated as a unique item. The organisation thinks it has 10 different components. It has one — it just does not know that.

The consequences compound: overstock on one variant and stockout on another, because the system does not recognise they are the same item. Reorders are missed. Obsolete variants are discarded as redundant. In international operations, the same component has different part numbers in each country and is procured from different local suppliers — cross-location draw-downs almost never happen because no one can map the records to the same item.

Manual resolution is impossible at scale. SAP and Oracle consultants acknowledge the problem but do not fix it. Cindy's summary: *"They come in and say 'you have a data problem.' They don't fix it."*

The AI opportunity: ingest heterogeneous inventory records from multiple sources, identify functional equivalence between different part numbers, map them to a canonical master record, and present a clean, unified view of what the organisation actually has. This then directly feeds the next step: using that normalised data to identify which parts should be sourced locally on demand rather than held in physical inventory.

**2. Andreas Leitner / Chris (OMV, #15, #18) — "Inventory Management Should Be Fully AI-Based"**
OMV confirmed that AI-driven inventory management is a strategic imperative, not an optional upgrade. Chris stated directly: *"Inventory management should be fully based on AI."* He requires a 15% demonstrable business improvement before adoption — a specific, measurable benchmark that AI-driven normalisation and optimisation can be designed to hit.

**3. Douglas Garcia (Equinor, #44) — AI Already Optimising Physical Parts Logistics**
Equinor is already using AI to scan its entire production system, identify which platform has a surplus part that another needs, and dispatch an unmanned helicopter to transfer it. This is AI optimising physical inventory logistics between offshore platforms. AddManuChain's proposition is the logical next step: instead of moving physical parts between platforms, eliminate the physical constraint entirely — AI identifies the need, triggers a certified digital print, and the part is manufactured locally. Douglas confirmed this framing: *"I think it's going to be huge... it's amazing how it's evolving."*

**4. Jason Power (Noble Corporation) — The Biggest Unmet Opportunity: Predictive Maintenance → Supply Chain Integration**
Jason identified the single largest unaddressed gap in the market: predictive maintenance systems can predict when a component will fail, but the connection between that prediction and the supply chain — ordering the right part, from the right source, in time — does not yet exist in any integrated form. His exact words: *"No one in the business has accomplished that yet."* AddManuChain's architecture — sensor signal → inventory check → certified AM trigger — directly closes this gap.

**5. André Simha (MSC, #59) — Data Quality Is the Real Blocker, Not Technology**
André was explicit: AI, ML, and blockchain are not the bottleneck in maritime supply chain. Data quality across the multi-party ecosystem is. Third-party operators provide incomplete, delayed, and inconsistent data. Even with 200–300 IoT-equipped ships, the systems to act on that data are immature. This validates the "normalise first" strategy: before AddManuChain can trigger predictive sourcing, it must fix the underlying data. The AI normalisation layer is not just a product — it is the prerequisite for everything else the platform does.

**6. Dean Dalpe (Enbridge, #64) — AI as Interpreter, Not Decision-Maker**
Dean was specific about the role operators want AI to play: surface insights and flag anomalies, but do not make autonomous procurement decisions. The human procurement manager still decides. This is an important design constraint: the platform should present AI-generated recommendations clearly and explain the reasoning, with the human approving the action. This matches the "AI as data interpreter" positioning that resonates across the interview series.

**The AI Product Architecture (As Validated Across 56 Interviews):**

```
LAYER 1 — DATA NORMALISATION (Phase 1 — standalone commercial value)
  Input:  Messy multi-source inventory records (SAP, Oracle, manual, multi-language)
  Output: Canonical master parts list — "you have 1 alternator, not 8"
  Value:  Eliminate overstock, prevent stockouts, enable cross-location draw-downs
  Customer: Any mid-size manufacturer or operator with a legacy ERP problem

LAYER 2 — INTELLIGENT SOURCING DECISION (Phase 2)
  Input:  Normalised inventory + part criticality + lead time + location data
  Output: "Stock this. Print that. Order this from here."
  Value:  Eliminate emergency sourcing delays; reduce physical inventory floor area
  Customer: Operators with field operations far from suppliers

LAYER 3 — PREDICTIVE SOURCING TRIGGER (Phase 3 — full platform)
  Input:  IoT sensor data → predictive maintenance failure signal
  Output: Automatic sourcing trigger → certified AM job order → Digital Birth Certificate
  Value:  Zero unplanned downtime. The part arrives before the failure.
  Customer: Asset-intensive operators with IoT-equipped equipment
```
