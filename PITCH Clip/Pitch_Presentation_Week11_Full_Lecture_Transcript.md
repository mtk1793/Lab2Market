## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF KEY LEARNINGS

### The Core Pitch Principle
**"Make people dream the same dream that you do."** — Guy Kawasaki (referenced by Joanna)

Pitching is not about explaining your technology or idea. It's about **bringing an audience on your journey**, getting them excited about your vision, and helping them understand why they should care.

### The Five Critical Questions Every Pitcher Must Answer
1. **Why are you creating this pitch?** (What's your goal — investor capital, partnership, customer, Lab2Market completion?)
2. **Who are you pitching to?** (Understand their perspective, needs, and what they're evaluating for)
3. **When are you pitching in the business lifecycle?** (Incubation vs. Series A vs. mature market — metrics shown should reflect this)
4. **How are you pitching?** (Virtual, in-person, board room, stage — context changes delivery)
5. **How much time do you have?** (3 minutes, 8-10 minutes, 20 minutes — affects scope and pacing)

### The Three Acts of Storytelling
All great pitches follow a three-act structure (loosely):

| Act | Component | Content |
|-----|-----------|---------|
| **Act 1: Setup** | Protagonist, Goal, Obstacle | Who is the hero (customer)? What do they want? What stands in their way? **Problem + Customer Focus** |
| **Act 2: Drama** | Challenge, Breakthrough, Sacrifice | What's the key insight (your solution)? What's the opportunity? What trade-offs exist? **Solution + Opportunity** |
| **Act 3: Resolution** | New World, Change, Call to Action | What does the future look like? How do we inspire the audience? How do they join the journey? **Roadmap + Vision** |

**Important:** The structure is not prescriptive. You can reorder slides, blend acts, and be creative as long as the *story logic* makes sense and keeps the audience engaged.

### The Attunement Principle
**Attunement = the capacity to take on another's perspective, understand their interests, and see the world from their point of view.**

Most pitching failures stem from entrepreneurs being "married to their idea" — so focused on the brilliance of their technology that they forget to speak to what the **audience cares about**.

**The Grandma Rule:** If your grandmother (or someone unfamiliar with your field) can't understand what you're pitching, your audience won't either. Avoid jargon, keep language simple, and always ask: "Why does this matter to *them*?"

Different audiences need different angles on the same idea. You should have *multiple stories* ready, but they all rest on understanding:

1. **Who am I pitching to?**
2. **What are they looking for?**
3. **How does my business solve their problem in a way they care about?**

- Identifying what works
- Adjusting your story for different audiences
- Gaining confidence

### PART 3: ATTUNEMENT & PERSPECTIVE-TAKING (04:08–08:38)

**The Central Principle:**

Pitching is fundamentally about **understanding your audience's perspective** so you can tell a story they care about. Most entrepreneurs fail at pitching because they:

1. **Are married to their idea/technology** — They believe it's objectively brilliant and assume everyone will see that
2. **Have limited perspective on their audience** — They don't think about what the listener actually wants or values
3. **Use jargon and complexity** — They explain the technical research instead of the human impact
4. **Misunderstand the audience's role** — An investor bets on the founder AND the idea. A customer needs to know: does this solve my problem? A partner needs to understand: what's in this for me?

**The Iceberg Metaphor:**
- **Above the waterline (visible):** Problem, technology, innovation details
- **Below the waterline (hidden, but much larger):** Beliefs, priorities, real pains, real gains, risk tolerance, innovation challenges

**To connect with an audience, you must address what's below the waterline — the human needs, not just the technical specs.**

### On Telling Stories vs. Presentations
Stories **land differently** than traditional presentations because:
- Stories simplify abstract/complex concepts (research, technology, innovation)
- Stories engage the human experience, not just the intellect
- Stories create emotional connection, which leads to action
- Stories show *why* a solution matters, not just what it is

**The Grandma Rule (super important):**
If your grandmother can't understand your pitch, most of your audience won't either. When you use overly technical language:
- People tune out
- They think the idea is not for them
- They lose interest

You can dive into technical details in Q&A, but in your pitch, speak in terms a non-expert can follow.

### PART 4: THE FIVE CRITICAL QUESTIONS (15:17–22:24)

Every entrepreneur preparing a pitch should answer these five questions first:

#### **Question 1: Why Are You Creating This Pitch?**
- Are you trying to finish Lab2Market? (Share progress, celebrate)
- Are you trying to get investors? (Seeking capital)
- Are you trying to sign a customer? (Close a deal)
- Are you trying to find partners or advisors? (Build your ecosystem)

**Why it matters:** Your "why" changes your pitch message. An investor pitch emphasizes growth and capital needs. A customer pitch emphasizes problem-solving and value. A Lab2Market pitch emphasizes learning and traction.

#### **Question 2: Who Are You Pitching To?**
- Are they technical or non-technical?
- Do they have experience in your space?
- What are they evaluating for? (Return? Impact? Strategic fit?)
- What do they care most about? (Revenue, team, market size, technology, social impact?)

**Why it matters:** Your pitch should be tailored to their perspective. An engineer cares about how the technology works. A business development person cares about go-to-market strategy. A customer cares about whether it solves their problem.

#### **Question 3: When Are You Pitching in Your Business Lifecycle?**
- **Incubation stage** (where all Lab2Market participants are): Show customer research, market validation, and evidence of real opportunity — not revenue yet, but proof of a real problem and a viable solution
- **Series A / growth stage**: Show traction (customer commits, revenue, unit economics)
- **Mature stage**: Show profitability, competitive moat, scale plans

**Why it matters:** The metrics investors/partners look for change based on where you are. At incubation, we're not looking at revenue — we're looking at:
- How many customer interviews/validation touchpoints?
- Letters of intent?
- Pilot agreements?
- Email signups or expression of interest?

All of these signal market pull without requiring revenue.

#### **Question 4: How Are You Pitching?**
- **Virtual (Zoom):** Time limit respected, Q&A available
- **Live on stage:** Scripted, time-limited, less negotiable
- **Board room conversation:** Deck already received; you're having a discussion, not a formal presentation
- **Elevator pitch:** 30 seconds, verbal only, highest-level message only

**Why it matters:** Your preparation and delivery should match the context. A stage pitch is tight and polished. A board room pitch is conversational and flexible.

#### **Question 5: How Much Time Do You Have?**
- **3 minutes:** Just problem → solution → call to action
- **8-10 minutes** (Lab2Market final pitch format): Enough to tell a full story with some detail
- **20+ minutes:** Deeper dive, multiple sections, room for Q&A

**Why it matters:** Time constraint forces you to prioritize. In a 3-minute pitch, you aim to get problem + solution + key insight into the first 1-2 minutes to hook the audience. Go too long before revealing what your business actually is, and you lose people.

### PART 5: STORYTELLING FRAMEWORK — THREE ACTS (23:58–35:15)

**Joanna presents the story structure template:**

### **Act One: Setup**
**Components:**
- **Protagonist:** Who is the hero of your story? (Your customer, a company with a problem, society/the world facing a challenge)
- **Goal:** What do they want? What are they trying to achieve?
- **Obstacle:** What stands in their way?

**Outcome:** Audience understands the character and their problem.

**Example for AddManuChain:**
- *Protagonist:* A mining operation on a remote site
- *Goal:* Keep their mill running 24/7, minimize downtime
- *Obstacle:* A critical part fails; it takes 6 months to ship a replacement from OEM

### **Act Two: Drama of Discovery**
**Components:**
- **Challenge / Issues:** What does the protagonist face? What are the breakthrough insights?
- **Technology / Solution / Business Model:** What's the innovation? How do you solve it?
- **Sacrifice / Price to Pay:** What trade-offs exist? What's the cost of this solution?

**Outcome:** Audience sees the opportunity and understands your differentiation.

**Example for AddManuChain:**
- *Challenge:* Global supply chains are fragmented; remote sites can't access parts
- *Insight:* 3D printing + certified local networks could print parts in days instead of months
- *Solution:* Platform connecting OEMs, print centers, and operators
- *Trade-off:* Requires certification governance, digital inventory trust, operational coordination

### **Act Three: Resolution & Call to Action**
**Components:**
- **New World / Future Vision:** What does success look like? What changes?
- **Growth / Change:** How do things get better?
- **Call to Action / How to Join:** What do you want the audience to do? (Invest, pilot, partner, refer)

**Outcome:** Audience is inspired, knows what the future is, and understands how they can participate.

**Example for AddManuChain:**
- *New World:* Mining ops can source spares on-demand from certified networks; no months-long waits
- *Change:* Supply chains become responsive, downtime drops 80%, inventory costs drop 70%
- *Call to Action:* "Join us as a print center partner," "Pilot this at your operation," "Invest in the ecosystem"

### PART 6: THE 10-SLIDE STRUCTURE (NOT PRESCRIPTIVE) (47:18–51:00)

**Joanna emphasizes:** The 10 slides are a *template*, not a prescription. The order is flexible. They're designed to flow through the three acts, but you can reorder them if it makes sense for your story.

### **Typical Slide Order (Loosely):**

1. **Title/Cover Slide** — Your name, logo, company vision
2. **Problem** — What challenge does your customer face? (Usually the hook — most engaging visually)
3. **Customer** — Who are you solving for? (Demographics, psychographics, pain points)
4. **Solution** — What's your product/service? (Visuals, name, core value)
5. **Why Now?** — What's changed in the market? (Inflection point, regulatory change, technology maturity, customer readiness)
6. **Market Size** — How big is the opportunity? (Use realistic, segmented TAM, not wildly inflated estimates)
7. **Business Model** — How do you make money? (This is crucial and often overlooked by tech founders)
8. **Competition** — Who else is doing this? (Show you've done your research; don't claim you have no competitors)
9. **Validation / Traction** — What evidence do you have? (Customer interviews, pilot interest, LOIs, revenue if applicable)
10. **Roadmap & Team** — Where are you going? Who's on the team? (Vision, milestones, team credentials)

**Key notes:**
- The Problem slide is often the hook — make it visually compelling and emotionally resonant
- The Business Model slide is critical. If you don't have a real way to make money, you don't have a business
- Validation slide shows you've done homework — not just an idea, but evidence of market pull
- Roadmap and Team inspire people to join your journey

### PART 7: PROBLEM SLIDE DEEP DIVE (48:01–54:46)

**The problem slide is your hook.** It's often the first real engagement point after your cover slide. How you frame the problem determines whether the audience leans in or tunes out.

### **Two Dominant Problem-Hook Approaches:**

#### **Approach 1: Micro-Level Personal Story**
- Start with a *personal experience* of the problem
- Make it relatable and specific
- Example: Bioluminescent light source founder started at festivals, saw massive plastic waste, got inspired

**Advantage:** Human, emotionally resonant
**Use when:** Your problem is tangible and experiences differ little across customer base

#### **Approach 2: Macro-Level Scale Problem**
- Start with *large-scale statistics* about the problem
- Show geographic, demographic, or sectoral scope
- Example: Plastics company showing plastic pollution stats (tons in ocean, impact on wildlife)

**Advantage:** Shows size of opportunity, credibility through research
**Use when:** Problem is less visible or deeply structural (pollution, disease, systemic inefficiency)

### **Hybrid Approach:**
Combine micro (individual story) with macro (scale of impact). Opens emotionally, validates with data.

### PART 8: WHAT TO AVOID (COMMON PITCHING PITFALLS) (41:17–42:45)

**The "Big Mistakes" List:**
1. **Not being yourself** — Entrepreneurs often try to look/sound like they think investors want, lose authenticity
2. **Not knowing your numbers** — You should know your metric better than anyone in the room
3. **Not knowing your stuff** — Don't pretend to understand aspects of your market/competition you haven't researched
4. **Not being prepared** — Fumbling through slides, not smooth delivery

**Carol emphasizes:** Investors are betting on *you as much as the idea*. They want to see someone competent, authentic, and capable of executing. Be yourself.

### PART 9: DESIGN & VISUALS PHILOSOPHY (51:00–57:58)

**Key Principle:** Visuals can clarify and engage. Text-heavy slides with small fonts will cause your audience to disengage.

### **Rules of Engagement:**
- **No paragraphs.** Ever. Slides are not documents.
- **Font should be large** (minimum 28 points for text that matters; some recommendations go even larger)
- **Visuals >>> Text.** Use:
  - High-quality photography (especially for natural/ocean/climate sectors)
  - Simple diagrams/flow charts
  - Charts and graphs (for data)
  - Videos (if relevant and short)
  - Icons and visual metaphors
- **Color & consistency:** Establish a visual brand (color palette, font family) and stick with it

### **Examples of Strong Problem Slides Through Visuals:**
- **Insurance problem slide:** Used bold visuals and simple language to show how insurance fails customers (referenced by Joanna but not shown in transcript)
- **Bioluminescent lights problem slide:** Visual of plastic waste at festivals + personal story = emotional + scale
- **Plastics/sustainability problem slide:** Macro statistics visualized beautifully for impact

**Bottom line:** Design your slides so they don't require you to read them. The audience should look at the visual and instantly get the idea. You then *explain* what they're seeing verbally.

### PART 10: BUSINESS MODEL SLIDE — CRITICAL (55:00–60:00)

**Carol jumps in hard on this:** *"Everybody wants to know how you're going to make money. This is the key analysis of why we're in business. Don't ever think we're not in business to not make money."*

**Even social enterprises and impact-focused businesses need a functioning profit-generating model.**

### **What the Business Model Slide Should Show:**
- How you make revenue (per transaction, per subscription, licensing, other)
- Who pays (customers, intermediaries, subsidizers)
- What the unit economics look like (cost per unit, pricing per unit, gross margin)
- Key partners and cost drivers

### **Why It Matters:**
Without a credible business model, you get asked constantly: "But how do you make money?" and it derails your pitch. If you lay it out clearly, you eliminate the question and show you've thought about sustainability.

**For early-stage companies:** You may not have revenue yet, but you should have *thought through* how you'd make money when you scale. That credibility goes a long way.

---

### PART 11: VALIDATION SLIDE (60:00–65:00)

**What counts as validation at the incubation stage?**

Instead of revenue (you don't have it yet), show:
- **Customer interviews conducted** (number + depth)
- **Letters of intent** (customers willing to commit once product is ready)
- **Pilot agreements** (willingness to test)
- **Signups** (email list, wait list, expressions of interest)
- **Press/media coverage** (proof of market interest)
- **Grants/awards won** (external validation of idea quality)

**Why it matters:** Validation de-risks the pitch. It says, "This isn't just my opinion — real customers have validated this need."

---

### PART 12: ROADMAP & TEAM SLIDE (65:00–71:00)

**Roadmap slide** should show:
- Timeline of milestones (Q1 2026, Q2 2026, etc.)
- Key accomplishments (completed prototype, first pilot, geographic expansion, feature release)
- Vision for the future ("By 2028, we'll have 50 print centers operational across North America")

**Why it matters:** Investors/partners want to understand your execution roadmap and track record of hitting milestones.

**Team slide:**
- Credentials of key founders/leaders
- Relevant experience (20+ years in manufacturing, PhD in materials science, serial entrepreneur)
- Gaps being filled (advisor board, hired talent)

**Important note:** You don't start with the team slide. You put it strategically later in your pitch once the problem and solution are clear. If you open with "Meet our team," you lose the audience before they understand why your team matters.

### PART 13: QUESTIONS FROM AUDIENCE (55:00–75:00)

#### **Q1: On Rearranging Slides and Story Flow**
*Participant asks: If we rearrange slides, do we mess up the story?*

**Joanna's answer:** It depends on what makes sense for *your* story. The acts don't have to follow the template exactly. For example, maybe the competition is *part* of your problem (existing players don't solve it). So your competition slide could come early. **The key is: does the reordering create logical flow and keep the audience engaged?** Test it. Put a deck together, play it around, get feedback. If it works, use it.

**Carol adds:** There's going to be someone out there who breaks all these rules and still kills it with their pitch. The point is: know the rules well enough to break them *intentionally for a reason*, not accidentally because you didn't think it through.

#### **Q2: Data & Background Information in Slides**
*Participant asks: Should I include detailed data in slides, or put it in backup slides and let them ask?*

**Joanna's answer:** Use common sense. If the data *illustrates your key message*, include it visually. If it's supporting evidence that complicates the message, put it in backup slides or have it ready for Q&A. **Does this slide look like a research paper or a pitch deck?** If it feels like the former, you have too much detail.

**Mark adds:** Define your 2–3 key messages and ruthlessly prioritize data around those. Don't try to jam everything onto every slide.

#### **Q3: Using Video in Your Pitch**
*Participant asks: Is it okay to include a 1-2 minute video showing my 3D printing in action?*

**Joanna's answer:** Absolutely, IF it's well-made and illustrates your key point. Don't rely on the video as your only communication tool — you still need to narrate. But a good visual of your technology working can be powerful.

**Suggestion:** Use it for the **problem** or **solution** slide where a visual really brings clarity. Shows credibility and engagement.

#### **Q4: Should You Introduce Your Team at the Beginning?**
*Participant asks: I'm thinking of opening with my team because I have relevant PhDs and experience. Should I lead with that?*

**Joanna's answer:** Lead with the problem and opportunity. Your team credentials matter, but they're not the hook. **Investors are betting on whether you're solving a real problem for real people.** They evaluate your team's fit *after* they understand the problem you're tackling. If you lead with "Meet our team of PhD researchers," people don't understand why your team matters yet.

**Put the team slide strategically later, once they're bought in to the problem.** Then reveal: "And we have exactly the right team to solve this because [credentials]."

## PRACTICAL INSIGHTS FOR FOUNDERS

### On Timing & Pacing
- **Get your problem + solution defined in the first 1–2 minutes** of an 8–10 minute pitch
- Don't spend 5 minutes on problem definition and then rush the solution
- Leave room for Q&A — don't use all your time on presentation only

### On Improvisation (referenced by Carol)
**If you're in a board room conversation (not a stage pitch):**
- Feel free to adapt your message based on questions
- If the audience isn't getting it, adjust how you're explaining it
- Flexibility and responsiveness win in 1-on-1 settings

**If you're on a stage (timed pitch):**
- Stick to your script
- Don't try to improvise mid-delivery
- Trust your prep

### On Handling Skepticism
When someone says, "We already solved this problem" or "Why do we need a new solution?":
- **Don't get defensive.** They're testing your conviction.
- **Acknowledge the existing landscape,** but explain why *yours is different or better*.
- **Anchor in customer feedback.** "In talking to 50+ mining ops, they told me the existing solutions don't address X because...</answer>
