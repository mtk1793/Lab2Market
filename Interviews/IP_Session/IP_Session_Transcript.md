## IP OVERVIEW

I'll move quickly through the slides. We're going to cover copyright, industrial designs, trademarks, then quickly jump to trade secrets — which ties quite a bit to AI — and software. I also have questions about other industries, so ask me all the questions. I'll be happy to answer.

**What is IP — Intellectual Property?**

We can call it an intangible asset — something that is a creation of your mind, something created within your company as an inventor, a student, a researcher. These creations of the mind are what we'll find protected here. It's not tangible property like real estate, but it occupies a space as property, and there are various types. Generally: copyright, industrial design, patents, trademarks, and proprietary information — well-known as trade secrets. Sometimes also recognized is what we call a GI — Geographical Indication. GIs are not recognized in many countries; they're associated to a region, not to a person. For example, a particular variety of rice that gets its flavor from the land and environment of a region — the GI belongs to that country or area. Canada doesn't have pure GI law, but we have conventional geographic indication.

## COPYRIGHT

**Kathal:** Copyright protects expression of ideas — not the ideas themselves. Broad categories include literary, dramatic, musical, and artistic works.

Key point: **copyright does not require registration**. It exists from the moment of expression. So if I draw a flying robot on a piece of paper today, I have a copyright that started today. I should still keep records and date them.

Some forms of IP require registration (and fees to the government) — patents, industrial designs, trademarks. Copyright does not.

But registration makes rights stronger. If you have funds, protect it formally.

**Lifespan:** In Canada, copyright lasts for the life of the author + 50 years. In the US, it's life + 70 years. That's why you still see royalties being paid to estates from old films and recordings.

**Why copyright matters for your business:**
- Instruction manuals explaining your patented product
- Ad campaigns, jingles, taglines
- Software code

Don't write it off completely — it can cover things you wouldn't expect.

**Important note:** Canadian copyright law is not as strong as some other jurisdictions, particularly the US where copyright is heavily litigated and enforced.

Now, some platforms are closed (they keep everything within your company's environment). Some are open — whatever you tell them, it uses to train the AI model.

**Key warning:** Whatever you feed into open AI systems, be careful — it may become part of the training data.

I also read an article this morning about conversations with AI not being protected the way legal conversations are. When you talk to a lawyer, there is attorney-client privilege. Your conversations with AI do NOT get the same confidentiality protection. This is important — especially for patent-sensitive information. If you disclose an invention to an open AI platform, you may have effectively made a public disclosure that could invalidate your patent rights.

**On hallucination risk:** Many firms are now using AI to assist patent lawyers. When I gave a random situation to AI and told it to draft a patent idea, it took my original idea and was okay — but it also formulated and added features that actually belong to someone else.

**Practical advice:**
- Use AI as a tool for inspiration and first drafts — not as the source of truth
- Vet AI outputs with an IP lawyer before filing anything
- AI is not recognized as an inventor in most jurisdictions
- Copyright in AI-generated content is a very grey area — there's a real risk that AI-generated content belongs to no one (no valid copyright), or that it infringes someone else's work

## INDUSTRIAL DESIGN

**Kathal:** Industrial design protects the **visual appearance** of a product — its shape, configuration, pattern, ornamentation. It does NOT protect functionality. Functionality is protected under patents (or trade secrets).

**Example:** A car. The technology inside — the airbags, the engine, the battery system — those would be patents or trade secrets. But the way the car looks from the outside — the unique body shape, the grill design — that's industrial design.

**Famous litigation example:** Samsung vs. Apple. Before the iPhone arrived (~2007), Samsung phones looked a certain way. After the iPhone became popular, Samsung's smartphones started looking very similar — to the point where a consumer could get confused. Industrial design law exists to prevent that confusion: protecting how it looks on the shelf, not what's inside.

**Registration:** Industrial design requires registration (unlike copyright). Canada has a very strong industrial design law.

**International filing:** There is a Hague System for international industrial design filing — you can file one application and pursue it in many countries at lower cost than filing individually.

## QUESTION (from audience)

**Audience member:** What's the real difference between an industrial design and a patent in terms of use cases?

**Kathal:** Very simply — I'll use a jacket as an example. If you're protecting how the jacket **looks** — the pattern, the lining design — that's industrial design. If you're protecting the **functionality** — how the fiber keeps you warm, the technology inside — that's a patent. And if it's a proprietary process that the company keeps secret, that's a trade secret.

Industrial design = the exterior, visible aesthetic.
Patent = the underlying technology and functionality.

## TRADEMARKS

**Kathal:** Trademarks protect the **branding** of your product. But it's not limited to just the company name or product name. A trademark can also be:
- A tagline (American Express's old "Don't leave home without it")
- A jingle or musical mode associated with your product
- A logo (black and white is often recommended — protects the shape, not just one color combination)
- In some countries: the shape of packaging (Hershey's Kisses), the scent of a product (Crayola crayons)

**Registration:** Trademark law does not require registration, but registration makes rights stronger. If you don't have funds to register, use your mark, keep records, and date everything — you will still have protection under common law (passing off). But registration is recommended.

**International filing:** There is the Madrid System for trademarks — you can file one application and designate multiple countries. Recommended if you plan to take your product to more than 2–3 jurisdictions.

**Question from audience (re: logo changes on social media):**

**Moderator:** If you update your logo — like companies do on Instagram — does the old trademark still apply?

**Kathal:** A changed logo would require a new trademark application. But you still own the rights to the original logo if you keep it. Many big companies maintain both the old and new marks. Some drop the old one entirely. If you rebrand, file a new trademark — but don't abandon the old one if you're still using it.

## PATENTS

**Kathal:** Patents protect **new inventions and processes** — functional improvements, methods, compositions. They are the strongest but most expensive form of IP protection. They also require full public disclosure of how the invention works (in exchange for a time-limited monopoly — typically 20 years).

Key consideration: **Patent vs. Trade Secret**. If your invention is easily reverse-engineerable, patent it (get the monopoly, accept the disclosure). If your invention is a process that can be kept secret and would be very hard to discover through reverse engineering, consider keeping it as a trade secret instead (no expiry, no disclosure required — but also no legal protection if someone independently discovers it).

**International filing — PCT (Patent Cooperation Treaty):**
- One application filed in Canada = stakes a claim in 150+ countries
- Gives you an 18-month runway to decide which countries to pursue individually
- Cost-effective way to buy time before committing to expensive national-phase filings

**Difference between PCT and Madrid:**
- PCT is for patents; Madrid is for trademarks
- PCT does not allow you to make substantive changes to your application after filing — only corrections
- You file a provisional application (placeholder), then have 12 months to file a full PCT application
- Madrid (trademarks) works differently — you designate countries at the start, and can update later

## TRADE SECRETS

**Kathal:** Trade secrets protect **confidential proprietary information** — formulas, processes, business methods, customer lists, pricing structures, etc. They have no expiry as long as secrecy is maintained.

Famous example: Coca-Cola's formula is a trade secret, not a patent — because patenting it would require public disclosure, and after 20 years the recipe would be free for anyone to copy.

**Requirements to qualify as a trade secret:**
1. The information must actually be secret (not publicly known)
2. The owner must take reasonable steps to keep it secret (NDAs, access controls, confidentiality policies)

**Connection to AI:** Be very careful what you input into AI tools. If you input a trade secret or an un-filed invention into an open AI platform, you may have just disclosed it — and lost your trade secret protection, or triggered a prior art event that could invalidate a future patent.

## KEY PRACTICAL TAKEAWAYS (as summarized by Kathal)

1. **Just having an idea is not enough** — express it, record it, date it, protect it
2. **Copyright is automatic** — but records help you prove it
3. **Industrial design, patents, and trademarks require registration** — start the conversation with an IP lawyer early
4. **AI outputs are not safe to copy directly** — always vet with an IP lawyer; risk of infringement is real
5. **Don't disclose your invention publicly before filing** — a single public disclosure can kill your patent rights in most countries
6. **Trade secrets can be more valuable than patents** — no expiry, no disclosure; but need active protection measures
7. **International filing is possible and more affordable than filing country-by-country** — PCT (patents), Madrid (trademarks), Hague (industrial designs)
